# All 191 Projected Rune-Poem Points — Human-Readable Form Grounded in the Poetry

This version redoes the corpus using the same general style as the earlier mistaken 24-point set, but now for all 191 projected poem points. The 24 die points are excluded. Each entry is organized by poetry rune and name, includes the original poem, the English literal rendering, and then a full human-readable interpretation written to stay close to the poem's semantic content.

## 1. ᚠ — Feoh

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Feoh byþ frofur fira gehwylcum;
sceal ðeah manna gehwylc miclun hyt dælan
gif he wile for drihtne domes hleotan.
```

**English literal rendering**

Wealth is a comfort to everyone; yet each person should distribute it generously if he wishes to gain honour before the Lord.

**Human-readable form**

The poem joins Wealth is a comfort to everyone and yet each person should distribute it generously if he wishes to gain honour before the Lord. Taken together, these images present value as something that attracts, organizes and disturbs. Wealth is pleasurable because it creates room, status, exchange and possibility, but it also pulls comparison and rivalry into the space between relatives, companions and competitors. The thing desired is rarely neutral once more than one person lays claim to it.

A second movement in the vector is the difference between usable wealth and guarded wealth. Treasure that remains connected to life can support freedom, choice and movement. Treasure that is hoarded too tightly becomes defensive. The image of the grave-serpent or of a dragon-bound hoard makes that danger explicit: value may descend into secrecy, fear and possession for its own sake.

This point therefore does not praise poverty and it does not romanticize accumulation. It asks what resources are actually doing. Are they supporting dignity, exchange, security and practical action, or have they begun to demand conflict, concealment and constant defense? The answer lies less in the quantity possessed than in the social and moral effects produced by possession.

The practical direction is to keep resources in use. Let them support life, reduce unnecessary dependence and make generosity possible without turning giving into domination. Wealth is strongest when it expands freedom rather than when it hardens into something that must be guarded at the cost of relationships.

## 2. ᚢ — Ur

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Ur byþ anmod ond oferhyrned,
felafrecne deor, feohteþ mid hornum
mære morstapa; þæt is modig wuht.
```

**English literal rendering**

The aurochs is proud and great-horned, a very dangerous beast that fights with its horns; a famous moor-roamer, a bold creature.

**Human-readable form**

The poem centers on The aurochs is proud and great-horned, a very dangerous beast that fights with its horns and a famous moor-roamer, a bold creature. The image is raw strength: a creature proud, dangerous and physically equipped to meet the world through force. The setting of the moor reinforces independence. This is not domesticated power; it belongs to open ground and its own momentum.

Such strength commands respect, but it is not automatically useful just because it is impressive. Something large-horned and bold may protect itself magnificently while remaining difficult to guide, share or live beside. The vector therefore asks what kind of power is present: social power, controlled power, or merely untamed capacity.

The point can be read positively as vigor, courage and unsoftened life. It can also be read as warning. Forces that are magnificent at a distance may become dangerous up close if treated too casually or admired without caution.

The practical lesson is to distinguish vitality from manageability. Respect power that truly belongs to itself. If you approach it, do so with realism. Not everything strong is meant to be possessed, and not every bold thing becomes wiser by being challenged.


**English literal rendering**

**Human-readable form**

The poem gathers images that remain partly unstable or obscure. That instability should not be erased. The vector indicates a real semantic field, but one whose surviving wording is damaged, compressed or resistant to exact translation.

Even so, obscurity is not emptiness. The point still points toward relation, condition or force; it simply does so through fragments rather than through a fully transparent statement. In such cases the right response is disciplined restraint rather than false certainty.

The practical lesson is to work with what is genuinely present. Let the surviving images set the limits of interpretation. A partially preserved meaning can still guide attention if it is treated honestly, without filling every gap with invention.

## 4. ᚩ — Os

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Os byþ ordfruma ælere spræce,
wisdomes wraþu ond witena frofur
and eorla gehwam eadnys ond tohiht.
```

**English literal rendering**

The mouth is the source of every speech, a support of wisdom and comfort of the wise, and to every noble a blessing and hope.

**Human-readable form**

The poem is built from The mouth is the source of every speech, a support of wisdom, comfort of the wise, and to every noble a blessing. Speech here is not chatter. It is the origin of communication, a support for wisdom and a comfort to those capable of thought. Language appears as a formative power through which knowledge is shared, organized and made socially effective.

Because of that, words carry more than information. They create bonds, confer dignity, preserve memory and make judgment possible. The vector implies that speech can be a blessing because it turns inner understanding into something that may circulate between people.

This also means speech carries responsibility. What originates language may comfort or wound, guide or mislead. Wisdom is supported by good expression, but distorted language can separate judgment from truth just as easily as clear language can unite them.

The practical lesson is to honor speech as a tool of formation. Speak so that understanding becomes possible. Use language to clarify, to sustain thought and to make genuine connection rather than noise. A well-formed word can do real work in the world because it helps shape the mind that hears it.

## 5. ᚱ — Rad

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Rad byþ on recyde rinca gehwylcum
sefte ond swiþhwæt, ðamðe sitteþ on ufan
meare mægenheardum ofer milpaþas.
```

**English literal rendering**

Riding seems easy to every warrior while indoors, but very strenuous to the one who sits on a powerful horse over mile-paths.

**Human-readable form**

The poem links Riding seems easy to every warrior while indoors and very strenuous to the one who sits on a powerful horse over mile-paths. Movement is the visible surface, but the deeper pattern is the division between the one who enjoys the journey and the one who bears its strain. The rider experiences direction, velocity and possibility; the horse or vehicle experiences effort, weight and repeated demand. Progress is therefore always relational.

This makes the vector useful whenever advancement depends on a support system. Careers, journeys, institutions and even personal ambition usually rest on bodies, partners, tools or conditions that absorb the cost of moving forward. The pleasure of motion can make that cost easy to ignore, but ignored burdens eventually interrupt the journey.

The point does not condemn movement. It asks for proportion and awareness. Good traveling requires attention to capacity, timing and the condition of what is carrying the work. Speed without regard for the bearer becomes self-undermining, while motion that respects the carrying force can remain graceful and sustainable.

The practical lesson is to move with what carries you rather than merely on top of it. Direction matters, but so do stamina and balance. The strongest journey is one in which the gain of motion and the cost of motion remain in conversation rather than splitting apart.

## 6. ᚳ — Cen

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Cen byþ cwicera gehwam, cuþ on fyre
blac ond beorhtlic, byrneþ oftust
ðær hi æþelingas inne restaþ.
```

**English literal rendering**

The torch is known to every living person by fire: pale and bright, it most often burns where nobles rest inside.

**Human-readable form**

The poem gathers The torch is known to every living person by fire: pale and bright, it most often burns where nobles rest inside. The central pattern joins durability with latent force. Whether the emphasis falls on the evergreen tree, on bow-shape or on brittle iron, the vector concerns a material capable of storing tension and releasing it under the right conditions.

There is an ambiguity here worth preserving. Yew may appear living and rooted or already fashioned into a weapon. In both cases, however, it belongs to a form of power that is quiet until drawn, bent or set alight. The danger is not always visible at first glance.

This point therefore favors disciplined tension. Strength is not only bulk or noise. It may be the capacity to remain green through winter, to hold strain without breaking, or to direct force through a carefully shaped instrument. What is resilient may also be dangerous once activated.

The practical lesson is to respect stored potential, both your own and that of others. A bent bow, an evergreen wood and a heated piece of iron all teach the same thing: endurance becomes meaningful when it can be directed. Tension is best treated as a resource to be governed, not as a condition to indulge blindly.

## 7. ᚷ — Gyfu

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Gyfu gumena byþ gleng and herenys,
wraþu and wyrþscype and wræcna gehwam
ar and ætwist, ðe byþ oþra leas.
```

**English literal rendering**

A gift is an adornment and praise among people, support and honour; for every exile who lacks other things it is mercy and sustenance.

**Human-readable form**

The poem gathers A gift is an adornment and praise among people, support and honour and for every exile who lacks other things it is mercy and sustenance. A gift is not only an object moving from one hand to another. It carries honor, relationship and the possibility of support. In the same action there may be adornment, praise, obligation and relief.

That complexity is important. Giving can dignify, but it can also bind. Receiving can sustain, but it may also create dependence or invisible debt if the exchange is not handled cleanly. The vector therefore asks not only what is given, but what kind of relation the giving establishes.

At its best, a gift strengthens social fabric. It acknowledges another person, provides what is needed and turns possession into shared stability. Especially where someone is isolated, deprived or cast out, a true gift restores more than material advantage. It restores belonging.

The practical lesson is to give in a way that supports rather than humiliates, and to receive with awareness rather than automatic submission. The most honorable exchange is the one that creates relationship without turning either party into a captive of the transaction.

## 8. ᚹ — Wenne / Wynn

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Wenne bruceþ, ðe can weana lyt
sares and sorge and him sylfa hæfþ
blæd and blysse and eac byrga geniht.
```

**English literal rendering**

Joy belongs to the one who knows little misery, pain, and sorrow, and who has prosperity, bliss, and sufficient protection.

**Human-readable form**

The poem revolves around Joy belongs to the one who knows little misery, pain, who has prosperity, bliss, and sufficient protection. Joy is defined not as excitement alone but as a condition relatively free from misery, pain and sorrow. It includes flourishing, protection and enoughness. Happiness here has structure. It depends on the reduction of what crushes life and the presence of what supports it.

This matters because the vector refuses shallow optimism. Joy is meaningful precisely because suffering is real. To know little sorrow is not trivial; it is a fortunate state. Bliss becomes substantial when secured by shelter, stability and the absence of constant harm.

The point therefore encourages gratitude without naivety. Enjoyment need not be ashamed of itself, but it should recognize the conditions that make it possible. Joy often rests on supports that remain invisible until they are threatened.

The practical lesson is to strengthen the conditions under which joy can last. Protect what reduces suffering, do not despise ordinary happiness, and remember that secure delight often depends on quiet forms of sufficiency rather than on drama.

## 9. ᚻ — Hægl

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Hægl byþ hwitust corna; hwyrft hit of heofones lyfte,
wealcaþ hit windes scura; weorþeþ hit to wætere syððan.
```

**English literal rendering**

Hail is the whitest of grains; it whirls down from heaven, is tossed by gusts of wind, and afterwards turns to water.

**Human-readable form**

The poem is built from Hail is the whitest of grains and it whirls down from heaven, is tossed by gusts of wind, and afterwards turns to water. The strongest pattern here is that the same force can nourish one part of life while frustrating another. Rain belongs to growth, but it also ruins cut hay, interrupts outdoor work and becomes hateful to anyone whose plans depended on dryness. The world is not arranged so that one condition is good for everyone at the same time.

Because of that, the vector does not speak mainly about fault. A person can prepare correctly and still be overtaken by the weather. Care matters, but care is not sovereignty. External conditions retain their own timing and force, and they often arrive without concern for the stage at which human work happens to stand.

This point therefore favors responsive judgment over rigid planning. Learn what in your situation depends on timing, exposure and storage. Protect what can still be protected, and do not waste energy treating every unfavorable change as a personal failure or a moral verdict. Some losses come from being beneath larger conditions rather than from making the wrong choice.

The deeper lesson is to work with conditions without imagining that you control them completely. Rain is not blessing or curse in the abstract. Its meaning depends on what it meets, when it arrives, and whether the living system in question required moisture or shelter at that moment.

## 10. ᚾ — Nyd

**Tradition:** OLD_ENGLISH

**Original poem**

```text
Nyd byþ nearu on breostan; weorþeþ hi þeah oft niþa bearnum
to helpe and to hæle gehwæþre, gif hi his hlystaþ æror.
```

**English literal rendering**

Need is constricting in the breast; yet it often becomes both help and healing to people if they heed it in time.

**Human-readable form**

The poem brings together Need is constricting in the breast and yet it often becomes both help and healing to people if they heed it in time. The core meaning is constriction. Need narrows the field of available options until preference becomes secondary and only what is necessary continues to matter. Hunger, cold, expense, obligation and hardship all belong to that narrowing movement.

There is pain in such reduction, but there is also clarity. Under ordinary circumstances a person can be distracted by too many possibilities. Under pressure, the environment itself reveals which choices are real and which were luxuries. Constraint is unpleasant, yet it can strip away illusion.

For that reason, this vector should not be read only negatively. Need is hard, but it can force honesty. It asks what must be done first, what support is truly essential, and what can no longer be postponed without loss. It makes priorities visible by limiting alternatives.

The practical direction is to respond to necessity with discrimination rather than denial. Separate what is essential from what merely seemed desirable when conditions were easier. To recognize constraint early is not defeat; it is the beginning of intelligent action under pressure.

## 12. ᛄ — Ger

**Original poem**

```text

```

**English literal rendering**

The year or harvest is people's hope when God, holy king of heaven, allows the earth to give bright fruits to rich and poor.

**Human-readable form**

The poem joins The year or harvest is people's hope when God, holy king of heaven, allows the earth to give bright fruits to rich and poor. The central pattern is ripening: conditions becoming favorable enough, and staying favorable long enough, for growth to pass into visible abundance. What matters is not sudden luck but completion of a cycle in which time, earth and care finally produce something sustaining.

This point is deeply material. A good year is known through grain, fruit, field and season. It supports people because the surrounding world has become capable of feeding them. Prosperity therefore appears less as private luxury than as a condition in which life can continue with less strain.

At the same time, harvest remains partly beyond command. Work matters, preparation matters, and skill matters, but a good season still depends on conditions larger than the individual. The vector preserves that humility. One may cooperate with growth without controlling it.

The practical lesson is to cultivate patiently, protect what is developing, and recognize abundance when it genuinely arrives. A ripe field should be used, shared and gathered in time. Growth is an opening, not a guarantee, and abundance has its full value when it nourishes more than the one who first receives it.


**English literal rendering**

Peorð is continual play and laughter among the proud, where warriors sit happily together in the beer-hall.

**Human-readable form**

The poem gathers Peorð is continual play and laughter among the proud, where warriors sit happily together in the beer-hall. Play appears as shared pleasure, laughter and the release that comes when people sit together in good company. The setting of the hall matters because this is not solitary amusement. It is social ease, ritualized enjoyment and the temporary suspension of strain.

Such play is not trivial. It helps create trust, mutual recognition and a sense that life contains more than labor and danger. The joyful atmosphere allows people to be present to one another in a less guarded form.

At the same time, the poem keeps the setting specific. This is pleasure occurring within a community capable of hosting it. The value lies not only in the game itself but in the environment that makes relaxed companionship possible.

The practical lesson is to respect forms of shared delight that renew the social body. Laughter, play and festival are strongest when they gather people without dissolving dignity. What seems like recreation can become one of the ways a community remembers how to live together.

## 15. ᛉ — Eolh-secg


**English literal rendering**

Elk-sedge most often dwells in a fen; it grows in water and wounds terribly, staining with blood anyone who grasps it.

**Human-readable form**

The poem is organized around Elk-sedge most often dwells in a fen and it grows in water and wounds terribly, staining with blood anyone who grasps it. Injury is the central fact. A wound is never merely the first moment of pain; it alters the condition of the body, leaves a trace, and may continue changing long after the event that caused it has passed. Where there is damage, there is also the possibility of worsening if the break is neglected.

This vector is particularly severe because it places injury beside vulnerability and decay. Harm is not abstract. It enters flesh, it affects the young, it persists after conflict, and it can turn into corruption if nothing interrupts the process. Pain here is diagnostic: it points to something that requires care, boundary or intervention.

The moral point is not to glorify endurance for its own sake. A painful condition may deserve treatment rather than admiration. In the same way, a conflict that has technically ended may still be present in its consequences. What remains afterward can be more important than the dramatic event itself.

The practical direction is to treat damage early, honestly and concretely. Name what has been broken, reduce exposure to further harm, and distinguish between healing and mere survival. What begins as a local wound can become a larger corruption if it is ignored for too long.

The practical direction is to use clarity as an active power. See accurately, persist long enough for frozen structures to change, and accept that visibility removes some of the shelter once provided by uncertainty. Real light comforts, but it also obliges.

# Center

**Human-readable form**

The center does not collapse into one single rune-image. It sits where many recurrent poetic structures overlap: weather and season, growth and decay, treasure and conflict, movement and burden, light and judgment, injury and remedy, animal force and human craft, water and boundary, need and sustenance. The center is therefore less a separate object than a balance point inside a living world.

One of the strongest features of this center is reciprocity between agency and condition. People act, but they never act in emptiness. Riders depend on horses, harvest depends on season, wealth alters kinship, wounds require care, law stabilizes shared space, and light not only reveals but also obliges. The center is not pure freedom. It is situated freedom.

The social dimension is equally strong. These poems rarely treat life as an isolated inner state. Joy, strife, gift, honor, inheritance, law, companionship and common nourishment recur constantly. Human meaning emerges through networks of use, exchange and responsibility rather than through solitary self-assertion.

At the same time, the center retains a deep respect for external reality. Rain falls whether plans are convenient or not. Ice supports while hiding danger. The sea exceeds control. Old age changes proportion. Mortality ends argument. This keeps the corpus from drifting into fantasy. Meaning remains tied to the conditions of embodied life.

The resulting practical orientation is neither passive nor domineering. It recommends accurate participation: use what supports life, protect what is vulnerable, move when movement is timely, accept hard limits when they are real, and keep resources and relationships from turning into forms of captivity. The center is the point where life becomes most intelligible as a constant negotiation between form and change.
