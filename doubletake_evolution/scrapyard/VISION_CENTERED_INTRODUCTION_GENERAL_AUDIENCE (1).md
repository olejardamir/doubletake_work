# 1. Foreword

This card system is different from ordinary cartomancy. It is not designed to tell a person exactly what will happen, reveal hidden facts with certainty, or provide instructions that should be followed without judgment. Its purpose is to help a reader look at a situation from several directions at once.

Human experience is too detailed and changeable to fit perfectly into any fixed model. Every situation contains context, uncertainty, personal history, incomplete information, and details that may matter only to the people involved. For that reason, the cards are meant to support interpretation rather than replace it.

The cards provide structure. The reader provides context.

A reading may help someone notice a pattern, ask a better question, compare possibilities, or consider something that had remained in the background. It may also produce associations that do not fit. Both are possible.

The present system is organized around one central concept:

## Vision

**Vision is the way a field becomes available to seeing, attention, and interpretation. It lies between openness and exclusion, movement and fixation, clarity and distortion. It allows enough awareness to register what is present, enough focus to distinguish what matters from what does not, and enough stability to maintain orientation as attention changes. From this center, perception can sharpen, widen, narrow, stabilize, wander, reveal, conceal, or disappear. Vision is therefore not a single image, but the condition that makes different ways of seeing possible.**

The 104 states of the deck are arranged around this center.

Here, *Vision* means more than eyesight. It also includes attention, orientation, recognition, concealment, emphasis, interpretation, perspective, and the way a situation is mentally organized.

The central question of the system is simple:

> **What becomes visible when a situation is looked at differently?**

# 2. Systems, Frameworks, and the Observer

The words **system** and **framework** appear throughout this book because they help define what is being observed.

A **system** is whatever you choose to place in focus.

It may be a person, relationship, family, organization, project, technical process, decision, social group, or another set of connected elements.

A **framework** is the surrounding structure in which that system operates.

It may include rules, institutions, physical surroundings, resources, technologies, expectations, schedules, limitations, environmental conditions, or other systems.

The distinction is practical rather than absolute. The same situation can look different depending on what you choose to treat as the system.

Consider a person changing jobs.

- If the person is the **system**, the organization, finances, labour market, and family obligations may form part of the **framework**.
- If the organization is the **system**, the employee becomes one part of it.
- If the decision itself is the **system**, both the person and the organization become parts of the surrounding framework.

None of these views is automatically correct. Each one emphasizes different relationships.

This is especially important in a Vision-centered system because observation itself changes what seems important. Something that is central in one reading may become background in another.

Before interpreting a spread, it is useful to establish three things:

1. **What is the system?**
2. **What is the framework?**
3. **Where is the observer placing attention?**

A card may then describe the system itself, the framework around it, an interaction between the two, a pressure acting on the system, a response from the system, or a change in how the situation is being seen.

Systems and frameworks are not meant as fixed divisions of reality. They are simply tools for organizing complexity.

# 3. How the System Is Built

## Why Playing Cards?

A standard playing-card deck is inexpensive, portable, familiar, and easy to use.

Without jokers, it contains:

- **13 ranks**
- **4 suits**
- **52 physical cards**

Each physical card has two semantic orientations: a primary state and a mirrored state.

This gives:

**52 physical cards × 2 orientations = 104 semantic states**

The two orientations are not "good" and "bad." The mirrored state is not a punishment, negative version, or simple reversal of the primary state. The two states are related parts of the same system.

---

## The Semantic Structure

The card meanings are arranged as a structured field rather than a loose collection of words.

Each card has a place in relation to:

- its rank,
- its suit,
- its neighboring states,
- its mirror,
- its Direct Opposite,
- and the central concept of Vision.

This is one of the most important ideas in the system:

> **A card meaning is partly defined by where it sits in relation to the other cards.**

That structure helps prevent the deck from becoming a set of 104 unrelated interpretations.

A state should make sense by itself, but it should also make sense as part of the whole.

---

## The Center of the System

The system has one main semantic center:

## Vision

During development, the card meanings were compared as groups rather than only one by one. Different ways of estimating the center of the deck led back to nearly the same semantic region, so there was no practical reason to create several competing global centers.

The system therefore uses **Vision** as its single global center.

This does not mean every card is literally about eyesight. It means the complete deck is organized around the broader problem of how something becomes available to perception, attention, recognition, and interpretation.

Different parts of the deck may still have their own local tendencies, but they remain parts of one larger field.

---

## Mirroring

Every physical card has two states: a primary state and a mirrored state.

Mirroring is not the same as finding an antonym.

The easiest way to understand it is to imagine the whole deck arranged around the center of Vision. The mirrored state of a card occupies the corresponding position on the other side of that center.

For example:

**A♥ Refocusing ↔ A♥ Defocusing**

This pair is easy to understand in ordinary language.

Other mirror pairs may be less obvious. That is expected. Their relationship comes from their position in the structure, not only from dictionary definitions.

A useful rule is:

> **A mirror is a reflected position, not necessarily an opposite word.**

---

## Direct Opposites

A **Direct Opposite** is different from a mirror.

Mirroring connects the two states of the **same physical card**.

A Direct Opposite connects **different cards** whose positions oppose one another within the deck.

The two relationships answer different questions.

### Mirroring asks:

> What is the corresponding state on the other side of the center?

### Direct Opposite asks:

> What is the opposing structural position elsewhere in the deck?

These relationships should not be treated as the same thing.

A card can therefore be understood through several connections at once:

- its own meaning,
- its mirror,
- its Direct Opposite,
- its neighbors,
- and its position relative to Vision.

The system becomes richer when these relationships are read together.

---

## Why Mirroring Is Necessary

Mirroring does more than double the number of meanings.

Without it, the deck contains only one side of the full structure.

The mirrored states provide another way of organizing the same conceptual field. They allow the system to represent situations that cannot be expressed simply by moving farther along the primary states.

Mirroring is therefore not an optional reversed-card convention. It is one of the main structural features of the system.

The two orientations should be treated as equally valid.

---

## The 104-State Structure

Each of the 104 states contains several layers.

### Core meaning

The shortest description of the state itself.

### Foundation principles

A deeper explanation of what the state represents and how it differs from related states.

### Toward relationships

Nearby or transitional states that help show how the card fits into the larger structure.

### Mirroring

The corresponding state of the same physical card.

### Direct Opposite

The state in the opposing structural position.

### Situational dimensions

Practical details that help the reader connect an abstract card meaning to a real situation.

The **Core meaning** and **Foundation principles** carry the main semantic meaning of the card.

The other sections explain how that state relates to the rest of the system and how it may be used in interpretation.

---

## The Situational-Dimension Layer

A card can have a clear abstract meaning and still be difficult to apply to a real situation.

The situational-dimension layer helps bridge that gap.

Every state is examined through the same kinds of questions. These include areas such as:

- Trigger
- Response
- Feedback
- Consequence
- Cause
- Effect
- Current state
- Emerging state
- Likely next state
- People involved
- Roles of people
- Relationship between people
- Intent
- Goal
- Motivation
- Attention
- Emotional tone
- Action
- Movement
- Environment
- Objects
- Animals
- Color
- Light
- Sound
- Temperature
- Time
- Risk
- Resources
- Constraint
- Opportunity
- Information
- Visibility
- Control
- Adaptation
- Recovery
- Primary obstacle
- Potential resolution

These are **dimensions**, not predictions.

For example:

- **Animal** is a dimension; a particular animal is a possible value.
- **Dominant color** is a dimension; a particular color is a possible value.
- **Trigger** is a dimension; a particular event or pressure is a possible value.
- **People involved** is a dimension; one or more social roles are possible values.

The values attached to a card are meant to help focus attention.

They should not be treated as proof that a particular person, object, animal, color, sound, or event must literally be present.

A useful rule is:

> **Treat a dimensional value as a question before treating it as an answer.**

If a card suggests a bird, for example, do not immediately assume that a bird must be present. Instead, consider whether ideas such as height, distance, watchfulness, movement, vantage point, or bird-like imagery might be relevant.

A literal match is possible. A symbolic match is possible. No match is also possible.

# 4. How to Read a Card

A card is most useful when read in layers.

### 1. Begin with the Core meaning

Identify the state itself before trying to connect it to a person or event.

### 2. Read the Foundation principles

Use them to understand the deeper idea behind the state and to distinguish it from neighboring concepts.

### 3. Define the system and framework

Decide what is actually being observed and what surrounds it.

### 4. Examine the mirror

Ask what changes when the same card is viewed from the corresponding position on the other side of the center.

### 5. Examine the Direct Opposite

Ask what the opposing structural tendency would look like.

### 6. Review the situational dimensions

Use them to generate concrete questions about the situation.

### 7. Compare the card with the rest of the spread

Repeated themes, contradictions, neighboring states, mirrors, and opposites often matter more than the wording of any single card.

The goal is not to make every detail fit.

The goal is to discover which relationships remain useful when compared with the actual situation.

# 5. The STEP-8 Spread

The STEP-8 spread is the main reading method used with this system.

Its most important feature is that the reader is allowed to rearrange the spread and then examine what those choices reveal.

The reading therefore includes not only the cards, but also the reader's response to them.

## Before beginning

Choose a situation or question and write it down.

Define:

- the system,
- the framework,
- what is already known,
- what remains uncertain,
- and what you want to examine.

Keep notes throughout the reading.

The purpose is not to force a final answer. It is to make the structure of your interpretation easier to see.

---

## STEP 1 — Establish the visible field

Place five random cards face up.

These are the first visible states in the reading.

Read each card in the orientation in which it appears.

---

## STEP 2 — Add the concealed field

Place one random card face down beneath each of the five visible cards.

Do not reveal these cards yet.

Each upper and lower card now forms one paired position.

If a position is moved later, the pair should move together until the lower card is revealed.

---

## STEP 3 — Observe before selecting

Read the five visible cards carefully.

For each one, note:

- what seems immediately relevant,
- what seems irrelevant,
- what catches your attention,
- which dimensional values appear useful,
- what conflicts with your expectations,
- and which associations feel forced.

An interpretation that feels forced may be telling you that the card is irrelevant, the question is poorly framed, or you are trying too hard to make the reading fit.

---

## STEP 4 — Choose the main three-card sequence

Select three of the five visible positions to form the main sequence and set the other two aside.

The simplest reading is:

- **left:** past or prior condition,
- **middle:** present or central condition,
- **right:** future or emerging condition.

These labels are practical rather than absolute. You may use another three-part structure if it suits the question better, as long as you apply it consistently.

If you move a visible card, move its concealed partner with it.

The two excluded positions should not be discarded. They become important later.

---

## STEP 5 — Reveal the three underlying cards

Turn over the concealed cards beneath the three selected positions.

You now have a visible three-card sequence with a second three-card layer beneath it.

The lower cards may be read as:

- underlying conditions,
- alternate perspectives,
- concealed influences,
- competing pressures,
- or additional situational information.

They should not automatically be treated as hidden truths. Their purpose is to complicate and test the first interpretation.

---

## STEP 6 — Read the six-card structure

Read the three upper and three lower cards as one interacting system.

Look for:

- repeated dimensions,
- contradictions,
- mirror relationships,
- Direct Opposites,
- recurring sensory or situational values,
- changes in attention,
- and differences between the visible and underlying layers.

This is where the dimensional information becomes especially useful because it can turn abstract meanings into concrete questions.

---

## STEP 7 — Examine what was excluded

Return to the two positions set aside in STEP 4 and reveal their concealed partners.

These four cards represent material that was excluded from the main three-card story.

They may suggest:

- alternatives,
- avoided possibilities,
- competing interpretations,
- peripheral influences,
- uncomfortable information,
- or states that did not fit the preferred narrative.

What you reject may sometimes reveal as much as what you accept.

---

## STEP 8 — Reorganize the field

Now examine the full arrangement and ask:

> **If I were allowed to change this spread, what would I change?**

You may switch cards, rearrange positions, or change which cards form the main sequence.

The goal is not to discover a single correct arrangement.

The important part is **your intervention**.

Ask:

- Which state do I want to remove?
- Which state do I want to preserve?
- What do I want to move closer?
- What do I want to separate?
- What state do I want to replace?
- What state do I want to introduce?
- Which dimensional values become easier or harder to accept?
- What changes in the story when I reorganize the field?
- What does that reveal about what I am trying to see, avoid, preserve, or change?

This is why the spread is called **STEP-8**.

The eighth step turns the reader from a passive recipient into an active participant.

In a Vision-centered system, this has a clear meaning: the reader is not only asking what appears in the field, but also observing how the field changes when attention and organization change.

The central questions become:

> **What am I treating as figure?**

> **What am I pushing into the background?**

> **What changes when I deliberately reorganize the picture?**

---

## STEP 9 — Record the result

Record the final arrangement and the reasons behind any changes.

If you repeat the spread later, compare the new reading with your earlier notes.

A useful reading may:

- expose an assumption,
- reveal a contradiction,
- generate a useful hypothesis,
- clarify a choice,
- highlight something that had been overlooked,
- or change the way the situation is being understood.

---

# 6. Why STEP-8 Is Enough

A card system can be made as complicated as someone wishes.

More cards, more positions, nested spreads, multiple decks, extra symbols, and additional rules can always be added.

Complexity does not automatically make a system more useful.

STEP-8 already contains many interacting elements:

- visible states,
- concealed states,
- selected states,
- excluded states,
- temporal or structural order,
- mirrors,
- Direct Opposites,
- situational dimensions,
- and the reader's own intervention.

The eighth step adds the most important variable:

**how the reader chooses to reorganize the model.**

That creates enough complexity to explore many situations without requiring increasingly elaborate mechanics.

---

# 7. Jokers

Jokers are not required for the 104-state system.

The complete structure already contains:

**52 physical cards × 2 orientations = 104 states**

A Joker may still be used as a deliberately defined wildcard, interruption, unknown condition, or special rule.

If Jokers are included, their meanings should be defined **before** the reading.

Changing a Joker's meaning after seeing the spread would make it too easy to adjust the system to whatever interpretation is preferred.

# 8. Historical and Philosophical Influences

This system developed through experiments with several traditions and conceptual approaches, including:

- ordinary cartomancy,
- Tarot,
- the I Ching,
- runic systems,
- symbolic divination,
- existentialist philosophy,
- systems thinking,
- logic,
- vector embeddings,
- and language models.

These sources influenced the design of the system, but they should not be treated as proof that the present system inherits the authority of those traditions.

## Existentialism and subjectivity

Existentialist thought was important in the early development of the project because it raised questions about freedom, responsibility, interpretation, and the difficulty of reducing human existence to one complete formula.

Thinkers such as Sartre and Heidegger helped shape the idea that people exist within conditions they did not entirely choose, while still interpreting, responding, deciding, and changing their relationship to those conditions.

That idea remains relevant to the Vision-centered version.

The deck provides structure, but it does not remove the observer.

A reading is not a mechanical extraction of truth from cards. It is an interaction between a structured field of meanings and a person interpreting a situation.

## Why historical divination systems mattered

Traditional divination systems demonstrated both the usefulness and the difficulty of symbolic interpretation.

Their meanings often contain layers of culture, translation, folklore, historical revision, and later commentary. Those layers can make a system rich, but they can also make it difficult to know why a particular symbol means what it does.

The present system does not escape culture. No language-based system can.

What it adds is a more explicit internal structure. A card meaning must work not only as prose, but also as one position within a larger set of relationships.

That does not make the system objectively true.

It makes the system easier to inspect, compare, revise, and test for internal consistency.

---

# 9. Scope and Limitations

The Vision-centered card system is intended for:

- structured reflection,
- observational exercises,
- creative association,
- perspective shifting,
- hypothesis generation,
- and examining how attention organizes a situation.

It is not a validated method for establishing facts that have not been independently verified.

It should not be relied upon to:

- predict future events with certainty,
- diagnose medical or psychological conditions,
- determine another person's private thoughts,
- identify hidden people, objects, or locations as fact,
- replace professional judgment,
- or justify serious decisions without independent evidence.

The situational dimensions make the system more concrete, which is useful, but they also make it easier to find apparent matches after the fact.

If a card suggests a color, animal, role, object, sound, or event, the reader may later notice something similar and conclude that the card predicted it.

That conclusion requires evidence beyond the card itself.

The strongest use of the system is therefore not:

> **The cards told me what is true.**

It is:

> **The cards caused me to inspect a possibility I might otherwise have ignored.**

# 10. Working With the Deck

Before a reading:

1. Define the system.
2. Define the framework.
3. State the question.
4. Separate what is known from what is uncertain.
5. Notice where your attention is already concentrated.

For each card:

1. Read the Core meaning.
2. Read the Foundation principles.
3. Identify how the card may relate to the question.
4. Examine the mirror.
5. Examine the Direct Opposite.
6. Review the situational dimensions.
7. Turn dimensional values into questions before treating them as conclusions.
8. Compare the card with the rest of the spread.

During STEP-8:

1. Notice what you keep.
2. Notice what you reject.
3. Notice what you move.
4. Notice what you replace.
5. Notice what becomes figure and what becomes background.
6. Treat those choices as part of the reading.

Afterward:

1. Record the interpretation.
2. Separate what you observed from what you inferred.
3. Verify factual claims independently.
4. Keep interpretations that clarify the situation.
5. Discard interpretations that do not.

The Vision-centered system is ultimately about **how a field becomes organized for seeing**.

Some things become figure. Others become background. Signals sharpen or fade. Interpretations stabilize or break apart when viewed from another position.

The cards provide a structured way of moving through those possibilities.

**The cards organize possibilities. They do not decide what is real.**
