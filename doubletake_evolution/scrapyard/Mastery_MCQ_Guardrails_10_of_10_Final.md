# Mastery MCQ Guardrails — 10/10 Final

## Purpose

Use these rules whenever creating **single-correct-answer multiple-choice questions for learning**, especially when practice is repeated until the learner reaches a mastery criterion.

The goal is not merely to make a valid test. The questions must cause useful retrieval, expose misunderstandings, provide corrective information, and require the learner to demonstrate the knowledge again rather than memorize a previous question, answer string, or answer position.

A correct response should be evidence of the intended knowledge or reasoning.

---

# 1. Non-negotiable core rule

Every item must:

1. test one clearly defined learning target;
2. require one dominant cognitive operation;
3. have exactly one defensible correct answer;
4. contain plausible alternatives;
5. avoid clues unrelated to knowledge;
6. be answerable from the assigned learning material when the quiz is source-based.

The learner should reach the key because they know, understand, or can apply the target concept.

---

# 2. Source-grounding rule

When questions are created from supplied learning material:

- Treat that material as the authoritative scope unless outside knowledge is explicitly requested.
- Do not silently introduce facts, terminology, exceptions, interpretations, or corrections that the source does not support.
- Preserve important terminology and distinctions used by the source.
- Every correct answer must be traceable to the source.
- Every distractor must be demonstrably incorrect under the source and the conditions stated in the question.
- Do not create questions about incidental formatting, examples, names, dates, or trivia unless those details are genuinely learning targets.
- If the source is ambiguous, incomplete, internally inconsistent, or insufficient to support one unique answer, do not invent certainty. Revise or omit the item.

For each authored item, be able to identify the source concept or passage that justifies the key.

---

# 3. Build a coverage map before writing questions

Before generating a substantial quiz, identify the material's important learning targets.

Classify each target, where appropriate, as:

- **Must know** — central concept, rule, mechanism, relationship, procedure, interpretation, or decision.
- **Should know** — important supporting knowledge.
- **Nice to know** — peripheral detail that should receive little or no quiz weight.

Then distribute questions deliberately.

Do not let easy-to-write facts dominate simply because they are convenient to turn into MCQs.

For large material, ensure the mastery attempt samples all major learning targets rather than repeatedly testing only a small subset.

---

# 4. Define the item before writing it

Internally specify:

> Given **[information available]**, the learner must perform **[one cognitive operation]** to determine **[one proposition or decision]**.

Choose the intended operation:

- **Recall** — retrieve a fact, definition, relationship, rule, sequence, or concept.
- **Application** — use learned knowledge in a new but structurally equivalent situation.
- **Analysis** — distinguish, interpret, diagnose, compare, infer, or select an action from supplied information.

Do not infer cognitive difficulty from stem length.

Use recall when recall itself is the learning objective. When understanding or transfer matters, prefer application or analysis.

Do not combine unrelated learning targets merely to make an item harder.

---

# 5. Question-stem rules

The stem must contain the actual problem.

Use the shortest stem that contains everything necessary to decide the answer.

The learner should ideally understand what is being asked **before reading the options**.

Rules:

- State the decision axis explicitly: cause, mechanism, calculation, classification, interpretation, consequence, next action, exception, etc.
- Remove decorative stories, irrelevant names, unnecessary numbers, and extraneous reading load.
- Use direct language appropriate to the learner.
- Do not turn reading difficulty into a hidden second test unless language comprehension is itself the target.
- Avoid trick wording.
- Prefer positive wording.
- Use **NOT**, **EXCEPT**, or other negative forms only when recognizing an exception, absence, contraindication, or non-example is itself important.
- If negative wording is unavoidable, make the negative unmistakable.
- Do not include information in the stem that effectively gives away the answer.
- For application or analysis, use a fresh surface example requiring retrieval and use of the underlying principle.

A learner who knows the material should not lose the item because the author wrote an unnecessarily confusing question.

---

# 6. Correct-answer rules

There must be **exactly one correct answer**.

Do not use:

- select-all-that-apply;
- multiple-correct-answer items;
- combination answers such as "A and C";
- "all of the above";
- formats in which more than one option can reasonably satisfy the stem.

Write the correct answer before constructing distractors.

The key must:

- be correct under the exact conditions stated;
- require no hidden assumption;
- answer exactly what was asked;
- match the specificity and abstraction level of the alternatives;
- be concise and canonical when possible;
- represent knowledge worth learning.

Do not make the key conspicuous through:

- unusual length;
- extra precision;
- textbook-like phrasing;
- unique qualifiers;
- grammatical compatibility;
- formatting;
- repeated wording from the source or stem that the distractors conspicuously lack.

Before accepting an item, be able to explain both:

1. why the key is correct; and
2. why every other option is wrong.

If two informed readers could reasonably defend different options, rewrite or discard the item.

---

# 7. Distractor rules

Wrong answers are part of the teaching design.

A distractor should normally correspond to a **recognizable error model**, such as:

- common misconception;
- reversed relationship;
- cause/effect confusion;
- incorrect denominator or calculation;
- ignored condition;
- wrong sequence;
- superficially relevant fact;
- overgeneralization;
- undergeneralization;
- confusion between related concepts;
- correct principle applied in the wrong situation.

Distractors must be plausible **before mastery**, not absurd.

Keep alternatives homogeneous in:

- grammatical form;
- semantic category;
- units;
- scope;
- specificity;
- approximate length.

Never add nonsense merely to fill an option slot.

Avoid distractors that are wrong only because of obscure technicalities unrelated to the learning objective.

Avoid clueing through:

- one grammatically compatible completion;
- one moderate answer among extreme alternatives;
- absolute words appearing only in wrong answers;
- one option containing another;
- obvious semantic overlap;
- repeated stem wording appearing only in the key.

A learner who does not know the material should have to think; a learner who does know it should be able to distinguish the alternatives for the right reason.

---

# 8. Number of options

Default to **3 options**:

- 1 correct answer;
- 2 strong, plausible distractors.

Use 4 only when a third distractor is genuinely plausible and diagnostically useful.

Never pad an item with weak fourth or fifth choices.

Distractor quality is more important than distractor quantity.

---

# 9. Independence and cross-item cueing

Questions in the same quiz must not accidentally answer one another.

Check that:

- one stem does not reveal another item's key;
- terminology introduced in a later item does not solve an earlier one;
- two questions do not become trivial when viewed together;
- one option list does not reveal the answer to another question;
- duplicate or near-duplicate questions are not counted as independent evidence of mastery.

Repeated testing of the same target should use deliberate parallel variants, not accidental duplication.

---

# 10. Answer-position guardrails

Across a set:

- vary the correct-answer position;
- do not favor one letter;
- do not create recognizable sequences;
- do not preserve the key position across parallel variants by default.

Do not obsess over perfectly equal letter counts if doing so damages item quality. The goal is absence of exploitable patterns.

Option ordering should follow a meaningful order when one naturally exists (for example numerical, chronological, or procedural order); otherwise use an order that does not cue the answer.

---

# 11. Feedback must teach

After an incorrect answer, provide concise corrective feedback.

At minimum:

1. identify the correct answer;
2. explain the decisive reason it is correct;
3. correct the misconception or reasoning error represented by the learner's choice when possible.

Good feedback explains the **principle**, not merely:

> "Incorrect. The answer is B."

Keep feedback focused enough that the learner still has to retrieve the knowledge later.

Do not reveal the answer before the learner responds in quiz mode.

For a correct answer, extensive feedback is optional. A short reinforcing explanation may be useful for important or difficult concepts.

---

# 12. Do not confuse correction with mastery

An item answered incorrectly and then corrected has **not** been mastered on that encounter.

Do not count:

- repeated guessing until the correct option is clicked;
- eliminating options after each failed attempt;
- immediately answering the identical question after being shown its answer;

as evidence of mastery.

After correction, test the learning target again later.

Prefer a parallel item rather than an immediate verbatim repeat.

---

# 13. Parallel-variant rules

For important targets, maintain multiple equivalent versions when practical.

A good variant preserves:

- learning target;
- underlying rule or concept;
- cognitive operation;
- approximate difficulty source.

It changes surface features such as:

- names;
- values;
- examples;
- context;
- presentation;
- data;
- ordering where appropriate.

Do not call something a new variant if it merely:

- swaps synonyms;
- preserves a distinctive answer phrase;
- preserves the same key position;
- changes irrelevant details while leaving recognition sufficient.

The learner must solve the concept again.

---

# 14. Anti-memorization rule

Repeated practice must distinguish **learning the concept** from **learning the quiz**.

Therefore:

- vary question wording appropriately;
- vary answer order;
- use parallel variants;
- avoid repeatedly presenting the same distinctive distractors;
- do not always test a concept from the same direction;
- periodically ask the learner to apply the same principle in a different context.

If performance collapses when superficial details change, mastery has not yet generalized adequately.

---

# 15. Mastery protocol

For the standard mastery workflow:

1. Present a complete mastery attempt covering the intended targets.
2. Record responses before revealing answers.
3. Give corrective feedback after errors.
4. Identify missed learning targets.
5. Re-test those targets later, preferably with parallel variants.
6. Generate the next complete attempt without exploitable answer patterns.
7. Continue until the learner earns **100% on one complete attempt**.

A question eventually answered correctly after repeated guesses does not count toward the 100% criterion for that attempt.

For high-value material, strengthen the criterion when practical:

> **100% on a fresh complete attempt using substantially unseen parallel items.**

For retention-critical learning, use an additional delayed mastery check rather than assuming immediate 100% guarantees durable retention.

---

# 16. Delayed retention and spacing

Immediate mastery and durable retention are different.

When the learning goal extends beyond the current session:

- re-test important targets after a delay;
- use retrieval rather than passive rereading;
- use fresh or partially fresh variants;
- focus subsequent practice more heavily on targets that were previously missed or fragile.

A delayed check should not simply reproduce the exact questions from the original session.

The precise delay should match the learning context; these guardrails do not prescribe one universal interval.

---

# 17. Difficulty rules

Difficulty must come from the intended knowledge or reasoning.

Do **not** manufacture difficulty through:

- ambiguity;
- rare vocabulary;
- irrelevant details;
- double negatives;
- misleading phrasing;
- microscopic distinctions outside the objective;
- implausibly similar options differentiated by trivia;
- time pressure unless speed itself matters.

It is normal for an item to become easy after learning.

The problem is an item that is easy **before** mastery because clues or weak distractors allow test-wise guessing.

---

# 18. Balance recall, application, and analysis

Do not automatically turn every sentence of source material into a recall question.

Match the question to the learning objective.

Examples:

- If a definition must be remembered precisely, recall is appropriate.
- If a rule must be used, ask the learner to apply it.
- If concepts must be distinguished, construct alternatives that expose the distinction.
- If a procedure must guide a decision, present the information needed for that decision.

A mastery quiz should represent what the learner is supposed to be able to **do with the knowledge**, not merely what is easiest for the author to ask.

---

# 19. Pre-release item quality check

Every item must pass all applicable checks:

- [ ] The learning target is specific and important.
- [ ] The item is supported by the assigned source material when source-based.
- [ ] The intended recall, application, or analysis is actually required.
- [ ] The stem contains all necessary conditions.
- [ ] Irrelevant material has been removed.
- [ ] The learner can understand what must be decided before inspecting the options.
- [ ] Exactly one answer is defensibly correct.
- [ ] The key requires no unstated assumption.
- [ ] The key is not conspicuous through wording, length, grammar, precision, or formatting.
- [ ] Every distractor is plausible before mastery.
- [ ] Every distractor is wrong for a substantive reason.
- [ ] Important distractors correspond to recognizable misconceptions or errors when possible.
- [ ] Options match in grammar, category, scope, units, specificity, and approximate length.
- [ ] There are no filler distractors.
- [ ] There is no all-of-the-above, combination-answer, or multiple-correct format.
- [ ] The item is not needlessly tricky.
- [ ] No other item in the set reveals the answer.
- [ ] The answer position contributes no useful clue.
- [ ] If this target appeared previously, the new version requires solving it again rather than recognizing wording or position.
- [ ] A knowledgeable reviewer could explain why every non-key option is wrong.

If any critical check fails, revise or discard the item.

---

# 20. Whole-quiz quality check

Before releasing a quiz set:

- [ ] Major learning targets are represented.
- [ ] Question allocation reflects importance rather than convenience.
- [ ] The set is not dominated by trivial recall unless recall is the intended objective.
- [ ] Important application/analysis targets are tested as application/analysis.
- [ ] There are no accidental duplicate items.
- [ ] There is no cross-item cueing.
- [ ] Correct-answer positions are not predictably patterned.
- [ ] Parallel variants do not merely reproduce previous wording.
- [ ] The total quiz length is sufficient to sample the intended material without unnecessary repetition.
- [ ] The quiz can support a complete 100% mastery attempt.

---

# 21. Post-use quality improvement

When response data are available, use them to improve the question bank.

Review items that show signs such as:

- almost nobody selecting a distractor;
- knowledgeable learners repeatedly choosing the same supposedly wrong option;
- unusually high error rates caused by wording rather than content;
- an item being answered correctly despite poor understanding;
- a distractor functioning as an obvious joke or giveaway;
- repeated learner feedback that two options are defensible.

Revise or remove malfunctioning items.

A distractor that nobody plausibly chooses is a candidate for replacement.

Do not preserve a bad question merely because it has already been used.

---

# 22. Separation of learner view and author view

## Learner view

Show only what is needed for learning:

- question;
- answer options;
- response interface;
- corrective feedback at the appropriate time.

Do not expose:

- answer key before response;
- distractor diagnostic labels;
- internal learning-target tags;
- item-writing notes;
- answer-position balancing logic.

## Author view

When useful, maintain:

- learning-target ID;
- source location;
- cognitive level;
- correct answer;
- rationale;
- distractor error model;
- variant family;
- item status or revision notes.

This metadata makes systematic mastery practice easier without giving clues to the learner.

---

# 23. Default generation contract

When asked to create mastery MCQs without additional instructions, automatically use these defaults:

- Single-correct-answer format only.
- 3 options per item by default.
- One clear learning target per item.
- Source-ground all items when learning material is supplied.
- Build coverage from important learning targets rather than sentence-by-sentence trivia.
- Use recall only when recall is the actual objective.
- Otherwise prefer appropriate application or analysis.
- Use plausible misconception-based distractors.
- Prevent cross-item cueing.
- Vary correct-answer positions without creating artificial patterns.
- Do not reveal answers before responses in quiz mode.
- After an error, provide the correct answer plus concise explanatory feedback.
- Re-test the missed target later, preferably with a parallel variant.
- Do not count immediate corrected responses as mastery.
- Require 100% on a complete attempt to finish the standard mastery cycle.
- Prefer a fresh complete parallel attempt for stronger evidence of mastery when practical.
- For durable retention goals, add a delayed retrieval check.
- In authoring mode, retain rationale and distractor diagnostics internally when useful; do not show them to the learner unless requested.

---

# 24. Default interaction protocol

If the user says:

> **Make mastery MCQs from this material.**

Interpret it as:

1. identify the important learning targets in the supplied material;
2. create high-quality single-correct-answer questions using these guardrails;
3. present questions without revealing the keys;
4. collect the learner's responses;
5. give corrective feedback for errors;
6. track missed learning targets;
7. re-test them later using parallel variants where possible;
8. repeat complete mastery attempts until the learner scores 100%;
9. use fresh variants for stronger mastery confirmation when practical.

---

# 25. Interpretation of 100%

A perfect score is a **practical criterion for the sampled material**.

It is not proof of:

- literally complete knowledge;
- permanent retention;
- flawless transfer to every new context.

The strongest practical evidence comes from successful retrieval across varied items and, when important, successful retrieval again after a delay.

---

# 26. Evidence and design basis

These guardrails synthesize established principles from educational assessment and learning research, including:

- one-best-answer item construction;
- alignment between questions and learning targets;
- avoidance of construct-irrelevant difficulty;
- plausible and functional distractors;
- retrieval practice;
- corrective feedback;
- repeated successful retrieval;
- mastery-oriented quizzing;
- resistance to item-specific memorization;
- use of parallel forms or variants when repeated exposure could otherwise produce recognition rather than learning.

Named foundations include the **Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing**, NBME one-best-answer guidance, research on multiple-choice item-writing rules and option count, and research on retrieval practice, corrective feedback, and mastery quizzing.

Not every item-writing convention has equally strong experimental evidence. Treat the most strongly supported learning principles—retrieval, corrective feedback, repeated successful recall, alignment, and avoidance of answer cueing—as higher priority than cosmetic conventions.

---

# 27. Final acceptance rule

A generated mastery-MCQ set is not finished merely because all questions have three options and one marked answer.

It is finished only when:

1. the important material is adequately represented;
2. each item measures the intended knowledge or reasoning;
3. exactly one answer is defensible;
4. distractors are plausible and useful;
5. irrelevant clues have been removed;
6. items do not answer one another;
7. repetition requires renewed retrieval rather than memorization;
8. feedback corrects errors;
9. the mastery attempt can validly require 100%;
10. the design can support later re-testing when durable learning matters.

If any of these conditions fail, revise the set before using it.
